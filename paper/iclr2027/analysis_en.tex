\subsubsection{Checkpoint Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:qwen4accuracy} reveals checkpoint-dependent effects. At Step150, Block3 improves 4B Base MATH500 Avg@8 by 1.85 points (pointwise interval $[0.70,3.00]$) and AIME24 by 2.92 ($[0.42,5.83]$), but reduces AIME25 by 4.17. At Step100, the MATH500 difference is $-1.40$ ($[-2.57,-0.25]$); at Step50, only AMC23 favors Block3. The official Instruct pair also reverses: Step50 favors Token on three tasks, and Step150 favors Token on AIME24. The intervention changes the learning path, not a fixed performance offset. We retain all checkpoints rather than treating their maximum as a validated selection rule.

\subsubsection{Block Length and Generation Stability}
Increasing the block width expands the set of cross-credit terms in Equation~\ref{eq:cross} and changes the joint-ratio clipping region. The historical Block10 runs show why this expansion merits behavioral monitoring: severe generation degradation occurs even though recorded optimizer diagnostics remain finite. Maximum pre-clipping gradient norms reach 867.50 and 865.21. The older built-in grader reports Step200 zeros on all four benchmarks, with a known AMC23 compatibility caveat documented in Appendix~\ref{app:history}. Thus the absence of NaN or Inf is insufficient evidence of healthy learning; finite but large gradients, repetition and answer failure must be examined together.

These runs do not isolate a single cause of collapse. Joint-ratio clipping, redistributed credit, prompting and rollout drift can interact. Random-shift and sliding-window variants were also evaluated at several checkpoints; their retained results are included in Appendix~\ref{app:history}. They are exploratory comparisons, not a completed factorial separation of advantage sharing, ratio granularity and loss scale. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} explains which source of phase variance a sliding estimator can remove without implying a general reward advantage.

\subsubsection{Prompting and Local Feedback Compatibility}
The first 4B attempt produces substantial repetition and frequent length-cap hits before its first optimizer update. This establishes that those initial defects cannot be caused by the block loss. The subsequent matched comparison uses a completion prompt without ChatML and separately derived request seeds. Since both changes were introduced together, the repair does not identify the individual effect of a chat delimiter. The bounded prompt probes in Appendix~\ref{app:probes} further show that Base and instruction-tuned models need model-specific input checks.

Under the repaired 4B protocol, training-cap incidence is 263/6,400 (4.11\%) for Token and 247/6,400 (3.86\%) for Block3. The official Instruct pair has 94/6,400 (1.47\%) and 77/6,400 (1.20\%) engine length stops, respectively, with no generated thinking tags. These low rates contrast with the severe historical degeneration, but do not imply universally improved formatting: evaluation differences in missing boxed markers and length stops depend on the benchmark (Appendix~\ref{app:instruct}). Prompt compatibility, length control and mathematical correctness measure distinct aspects of the training system.

\subsubsection{Credit Redistribution and Training Dynamics}
In the 4B comparison, Block3's median sign-disagreement rate across the 200 update summaries is 15.57\%, or 6.68\% when weighted by absolute local advantage. Its median relative redistribution magnitude is 1.148; this is a ratio of magnitudes, not a probability, and can exceed one. The corresponding size-one Token statistics are zero by construction. These measurements quantify the intervention rather than identifying one algorithm's feedback as correct.

Figure~\ref{fig:qwen4training} shows low late-training entropy and largely reduced gradient norms without the severe historical Block10 behavior. Nonzero credit redistribution therefore does not itself diagnose collapse. Position maps in Appendix~\ref{app:positionfigures} localize entropy and teacher--student overlap changes, while their occupancy masks distinguish measured positions from uncovered ones. Because on-policy prefixes change during training, these maps describe visited states rather than a fixed-input causal test.

The analysis distinguishes shared signals, clipping and loss scale; the experiments evaluate their combined implementation. Isolating these components and replicating across seeds and datasets remain necessary to identify the drivers of improvement (Appendix~\ref{sec:limits}).
