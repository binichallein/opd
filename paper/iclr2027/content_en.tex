\section{Introduction}
Transferring reasoning behavior from a capable teacher is an important route to improving language models. On-policy distillation (OPD) trains a student on its own responses, with teacher feedback at the prefixes the student actually visits \citep{gkd,minillm}. This brings supervision to learner-induced states rather than relying only on expert demonstrations \citep{dagger}. Teacher probabilities provide a dense learning signal throughout the response, making OPD a useful setting for studying how reasoning behavior is acquired during post-training.

The availability of dense feedback leaves a central design choice open: what should be the unit of credit assignment? A common sampled-token implementation assigns each token its own teacher--student log-probability difference \citep{revisiting}. This treats feedback at neighboring positions separately, even when several tokens jointly express a short mathematical operation or a transition in a solution. We ask: \textbf{can sharing teacher feedback across a short token block improve on-policy reasoning distillation?}

Our intuition is that a local decision can have several textual components. Their individual probability differences may fluctuate with the realization of the expression, while their aggregate can capture a shared preference. A short block provides an intermediate unit between a single token and a complete response: it allows neighboring choices to learn together while retaining local supervision.

We introduce \method{}, a block-level update that reuses the feedback already produced by an OPD teacher. After the student generates a response, we divide it into disjoint three-token blocks, average the distillation advantages within each block, and optimize a clipped objective with a joint block importance ratio and valid-length weighting (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). The procedure requires no additional reward model or process annotations. It changes how teacher scores guide the update, while retaining full-response sampling and the same teacher-scoring pipeline.

We evaluate on MATH500, AIME24, AIME25 and AMC23, with matched Token OPD controls and checkpoint-level reporting. In the historical Qwen3-1.7B comparison, Block3 improves Step200 Avg@8 by 8.90 percentage points on MATH500 and 5.42 on AMC23. The official Qwen3-8B-to-1.7B Instruct comparison also yields positive Step200 Avg@8 differences on all four benchmarks. To understand the update beyond endpoint scores, we connect its algebraic structure to training trajectories, including entropy, token overlap, gradient norms and local credit redistribution.

Our contributions are threefold. We formulate a local block-level OPD objective combining shared teacher feedback and a joint importance ratio. We derive its cross-token credit terms, gradient scale and clipping behavior, and characterize when local averaging improves a signal estimate. We then evaluate reasoning performance across teacher--student pairs and saved checkpoints, using trajectory diagnostics to connect the observed results to credit allocation and generation behavior.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
This study uses mathematical reasoning benchmarks and pretrained language models, without collecting human-subject data. Teacher imitation does not itself establish truthfulness or safety. We report both improvements and degraded outcomes, and distinguish mathematical accuracy from formatting and numerical diagnostics. Distribution of artifacts must respect upstream model and dataset licenses and exclude credentials, private model weights and identifying infrastructure information.
