\paragraph{Sampled local feedback.}
For prompt $x$, an old student $\pi_{\mathrm{old}}$ generates $y=(y_1,\ldots,y_T)$.
At prefix $h_t=(x,y_{<t})$, let
\begin{equation}
 a_t=\log\pi_{\mathrm{T}}(y_t\mid h_t)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_t\mid h_t),
 \quad r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(y_t\mid h_t)}.
 \label{eq:token}
\end{equation}
We stop gradients through $a_t$ and the old probabilities. An ``advantage'' here is a distillation log ratio, not a verified increment in task correctness. Teacher scoring remains autoregressive and can score an already generated sequence in a batched forward pass; grouping tokens does not remove the need to evaluate their conditional probabilities.

Write $\mathcal C(r,a)$ for the PPO clipped surrogate \citep{ppo}, with the inherited negative-advantage dual-clipping rule specified in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. Ignoring separately configured regularizers, the baseline averages $-\mathcal C(r_t,\sg(a_t))$ over valid response tokens. For exposition we use a single response with $T$ valid tokens; the implementation masks padding and reduces over valid entries in each micro-batch.

\paragraph{The implemented block intervention.}
Group consecutive response tensor positions into disjoint blocks $B_j$ of width $k$, without packing across mask holes. Below, sums within a block select valid positions, $n_j$ is their count, and $T=\sum_j n_j$. Empty blocks have zero weight. The ideal real-arithmetic reduction, omitting the implementation's $10^{-8}$ denominator stabilizer, is
\begin{align}
 \bar a_j&=\frac{1}{\max(1,n_j)}\sum_{t\in B_j}a_t,
 &R_j(\theta)&=\exp\left(\sum_{t\in B_j}\log r_t(\theta)\right),\label{eq:block}\\
 \mathcal L_{\mathrm{block}}&=-\frac1T\sum_{j:n_j>0}n_j\mathcal C(R_j,\sg(\bar a_j)),
 &\mathcal L_{\mathrm{token}}&=-\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T\mathcal C(r_t,\sg(a_t)).\label{eq:loss}
\end{align}
\method{} uses $k=3$. Importantly, $R_j=\prod_{t\in B_j}r_t$ is not an average of token ratios. In code, the block mask has weight $n_j/k$ and the denominator is the sum of these fractional weights; Equation~\ref{eq:loss} is its equivalent token-count form when the stabilizer is omitted. Full blocks reduce to an average over blocks; a two-token tail has weight $2/3$, not one. This objective uses sampled blocks, not enumeration of a $|V|^k$ distribution. Both arms generate complete trajectory batches before updating, rather than taking an optimizer step every three tokens.

\paragraph{Cross-credit and scale.}
Consider equal-sized blocks, $T=kJ$, at the old policy, where every ratio is one and clipping is inactive. Let $g_t=\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)$. The policy-gradient components satisfy
\begin{align}
 \nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{token}}&=-\frac1T\sum_t a_tg_t,\nonumber\\
 \nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{block}}&=-\frac1T\sum_j\sum_{i\in B_j}\sum_{t\in B_j}a_i g_t
 =\nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{token}}-\frac1T\sum_j\sum_{\substack{i,t\in B_j\\i\ne t}}a_i g_t.
 \label{eq:cross}
\end{align}
The additional terms send token $i$'s signal to token $t$'s score gradient. They can align or conflict with the baseline; their sign alone does not tell us whether downstream mathematical reasoning benefits.

\begin{proposition}[Equal feedback does not imply equal updates]
At $\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{old}}$, with inactive clipping, full blocks of size $k$, and $a_t=a$ for all valid tokens, both policy losses equal $-a$, but
$\nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{block}}=k\nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{token}}$.
\end{proposition}
The statement follows immediately by substituting $\bar a_j=a$ in Equation~\ref{eq:loss}. It concerns the policy-loss derivative, not a claim of a $k$-fold Adam parameter update or a $k$-fold total gradient after other terms and gradient clipping. It explains why a loss-value-only unit test can miss an implementation difference. As the policy moves, the joint ratio also changes the clipping region: for three token ratios of 1.1, the block ratio is $1.1^3=1.331$. A token ratio can therefore remain within a conventional 0.2 clipping band while its block ratio leaves it. Rescaling the learning rate alone cannot generally remove this difference.

\paragraph{When averaging could help.}
To separate the averaging hypothesis from the implementation above, consider a hypothetical token-ratio, token-normalized intervention using $Wa$ instead of $a$, where $W$ is deterministic and each row sums to one. Suppose, only as a local signal model, $a=\mu+\varepsilon$, $\E\varepsilon=0$, and $\Cov(\varepsilon)=\Sigma$. Then
\begin{equation}
 \E\|Wa-\mu\|_2^2=\|(W-I)\mu\|_2^2+\operatorname{tr}(W\Sigma W^\top).
 \label{eq:mse}
\end{equation}
Averaging reduces this error relative to the unsmoothed signal only if its bias cost is smaller than its noise reduction. With a constant signal and independent equal-variance noise within a block, the per-position variance becomes $\sigma^2/k$. With different latent signals or strongly correlated noise, this advantage may vanish. Equation~\ref{eq:mse} is not a theorem that Block3 or a sliding window improves reward: it concerns signal estimation, assumes a latent decomposition, and holds gradient features fixed. It also does not isolate the historical method's ratio and normalization changes. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives the derivation and a counterexample to unconditional smoothing benefit.
